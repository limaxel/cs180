# 1.1 Convolutions from scratch
<p>When I initially approached this problem, I looped over every output pixel and over every filter value. That worked, but it was extremely repetitive. My refined version still loops over pixels, but now multiples a whole image patch by the filter with NumPy. Both versions flip the filter first, so these are convolutions rather than correlations.</p>
<p>I applied the convolutions to a square crop of my portrait. To keep it within the NumPy-only requirement, I converted RGB to grayscale with a weighted array sum and made the smaller working copies with reshape-and-mean block averaging.</p>
```python
def conv_four_loops(im, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    top, left = kh // 2, kw // 2
    bottom, right = kh - 1 - top, kw - 1 - left
    padded = np.pad(im, ((top, bottom), (left, right)))
    flipped = np.flip(kernel, axis=(0, 1))
    ans = np.zeros_like(im, dtype=float)
    for r in range(im.shape[0]):
        for c in range(im.shape[1]):
            for kr in range(kh):
                for kc in range(kw):
                    ans[r, c] += padded[r+kr, c+kc] * flipped[kr, kc]
    return ans

def conv_two_loops(im, kernel):
    for r in range(im.shape[0]):
        for c in range(im.shape[1]):
            patch = padded[r:r+kh, c:c+kw]
            ans[r, c] = np.sum(patch * flipped)
    return ans
```

<p>I convolved the cameraman with <code>Dx = [1, 0, -1]</code> and its transpose. I then combined them with <code>sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2)</code>. My first threshold was around 0.1, but it kept most of the grass and camera texture. I ended at 0.29. It loses a couple faint edges, but the person, tripod, camera, and skyline are much easier to read.</p>

# 1.3 Derivative of Gaussian
<p>By intuition, the fix for the noise was to blur before differentiating. I built a 13x13 Gaussian using <code>cv2.getGaussianKernel(13, 2)</code> and an outer product. After smoothing, I could lower the threshold to 0.10 without bringing all the grass back. Edges are wider and rounder, but much less broken.</p>
<p>Doing blur then derivative sounds like two convolutions. By associativity, I can instead convolve the Gaussian with Dx and Dy once to make two DoG filters, and apply those directly. The maximum magnitude difference below is around 1e-15, so the two paths agree up to floating-point error.</p>

# 2.1 Image sharpening
<p>A Gaussian gives the low frequencies, so <code>image - blur</code> gives the high frequencies. Adding some multiple of that residual makes edges stronger:</p>

sharp = image + &alpha;(image - G * image) = ((1 + &alpha;)I - &alpha;G) * image

<p>The right side was useful because it combines everything into one unsharp-mask convolution. My first settings were too strong and made every edge crunchy. With &sigma;=1.0, alpha 0.25 is subtle, 0.5-0.75 makes the Taj crisper, and 1.5 already produces distracting halos. So "more sharp" is not always better.</p>

# 2.2 Hybrid images
<p>I got the best Derek/Nutmeg result with Nutmeg as the high-frequency image (&sigma;=4) over Derek as the low-frequency image (&sigma;=10). The whiskers, ears, and fur dominate up close, while Derek's broader face emerges after those details disappear at a distance. Nutmeg is tilted, so I marked both pairs of eye centers and estimated a similarity transform. This rotates, scales, and translates each image until the eyes land on the same horizontal line. I saved the landmark coordinates in the script so it runs without waiting for clicks.</p>
<pre><code>high = high_im - gaussian(high_im, sigma_high)
low = gaussian(low_im, sigma_low)
hybrid = np.clip(low + high, 0, 1)</code></pre>

# 2.3 Gaussian and Laplacian stacks
<p>At first I accidentally built a pyramid by resizing each level. A stack never downsamples, so every array stays 300x300. My other mistake was repeatedly applying the same tiny blur, where its effective width grows too slowly and left a visible seam. I fixed that by doubling sigma at each Gaussian level. For levels G0...G5, I used <code>Li = Gi - G(i+1)</code>, with the last blurry Gaussian as the last Laplacian level. Summing all the Laplacian levels reconstructs the input.</p>
<p>I implemented these stacks directly with Gaussian filtering and subtraction. I did not call <code>cv2.pyrDown</code>, <code>pyramid_gaussian</code>, or another built-in pyramid routine. The first panel group shows the complete Gaussian stacks for the original apple and orange. The later groups recreate the structure of Figure 3.42 at every level, with both source Laplacians, their masked contributions, the Gaussian mask, and the blended band. Signed Laplacian values are centered at gray only for display.</p>

# 2.4 Multiresolution blending
<p>For the required baseline, I begin with the original, unmodified apple and orange and a binary step mask, with white on the left and black on the right. I do not feather this input mask. Instead, its Gaussian stack creates increasingly wide transitions at coarser scales. Fine-frequency layers switch quickly from apple to orange, while coarse layers transition much more gradually. I multiply each pair of Laplacian layers by its corresponding mask level, add them, and finally sum the mixed layers.</p>
<pre><code>for i in range(levels):
    mixed_i = mask_gaussian[i] * apple_laplacian[i] \
            + (1 - mask_gaussian[i]) * orange_laplacian[i]
result = sum(mixed_levels)</code></pre>

<p>My first custom blend is a cheese moon. Instead of dividing the circle in half, I resized the square cheese crop over the entire moon and compared each cheese pixel with a heavily blurred local average. Pixels that are substantially darker than their neighborhood form a soft irregular mask for the holes. I matched the cheese texture's luminance statistics to the moon and gave both sources the same warm chroma. Multiresolution blending then inserts the holes across the surface while leaving the moon's craters and shading elsewhere. Finally, I restore the circular silhouette after reconstruction. For the other irregular-mask example, I cropped the upright snake and started from a cropped cord photo. I estimated a foreground mask from its nearly uniform whitish-grey background, removed the original lower snake with inpainting, and put the isolated cord onto that grass. I then measured the two silhouettes at the join and shifted/narrowed the cable until their centers and widths agreed.</p>

# What I learned
<p>The most important thing I learned is that the exact same basic operation can behave like blur, edge detection, sharpening, or blending depending on what frequency bands I keep. I also did not expect alignment and masking to matter as much as the actual filter. A mathematically correct hybrid can still look bad when its features do not line up, and a mathematically correct blend can still show a seam when the mask changes too abruptly.</p>
