# CS180 Project 4A - Image Warping and Mosaicing

I computed homographies from matching points, used them to rectify images, and stitched three sets of photos into mosaics. I also tried cylindrical projection for the wider scenes.

<!--more-->

# A.1 - Taking the pictures

I used three sets of overlapping photos from a building courtyard (The Gateway), an outdoor exercise area (Muscle Beach Venice), and a city overlook (from The Getty). The goal was to rotate the view from roughly the same camera position, although small movements and moving subjects can still cause alignment problems. The source photos are shown alongside each mosaic below.

# A.2 - Recovering homographies

<p>A homography maps a point in one image to a point in another using \(\mathbf{p}'\sim H\mathbf{p}\). The matching points for the main mosaics were selected visually using the <a href="https://cal-cs180.github.io/fa23/hw/proj3/tool.html">course point-selection tool</a>. I then computed \(H\) using least squares rather than a built-in homography solver. I kept SIFT as a separate comparison near the end.</p>

<p>With \(h_{33}=1\), each correspondence \((x,y)\mapsto(u,v)\) gives two equations.</p>

<div class="p4-equation">
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -ux & -uy \\
0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -vx & -vy
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{h} =
\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix}
\]
</div>

<p>For a concrete example, the first two saved matches for the building pair are shown below.</p>

<div class="p4-equation">
\[
\begin{aligned}
(796,\ 453) &\mapsto (414,\ 449) \\
(845,\ 451) &\mapsto (465,\ 449)
\end{aligned}
\]
</div>

<p>I center and scale each image's full point set so its mean distance from the origin is \(\sqrt{2}\). In these normalized coordinates, the first two matches contribute the first four rows below. The unknown vector is \(\mathbf{h}_n=(h^n_{11},h^n_{12},h^n_{13},h^n_{21},h^n_{22},h^n_{23},h^n_{31},h^n_{32})^T\), with \(h^n_{33}=1\).</p>

<div class="p4-equation">
\[
\small
\begin{bmatrix}
-1.0258 & 1.1524 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -1.0798 & 1.2131 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & -1.0258 & 1.1524 & 1 & 1.1577 & -1.3006 \\
-0.6532 & 1.1372 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -0.4281 & 0.7454 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & -0.6532 & 1.1372 & 1 & 0.7372 & -1.2835 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{h}_n \simeq
\begin{bmatrix}
-1.0526 \\
1.1286 \\
-0.6554 \\
1.1286 \\
\vdots
\end{bmatrix}
\]
</div>

<p>This is an excerpt of the \(40\times8\) system from all 20 matches. The displayed numbers are rounded, while the code uses full precision. I fit \(H_n\) by least squares, undo normalization using \(H=T_{\mathrm{target}}^{-1}H_nT_{\mathrm{source}}\), and rescale so \(h_{33}=1\). The fitted matrix below is for this building pair.</p>

# A.3 - Image rectification

<p>I wrote nearest-neighbor and bilinear samplers and used inverse warping. Instead of sending each input pixel forward and leaving gaps, I mapped every output pixel back to its source position with \(H^{-1}\).</p>

Nearest neighbor takes the closest pixel. Bilinear interpolation combines the four surrounding pixels using their fractional distances. I used the same homography for both methods so only the sampling changed.

## Accessible parking sign

I selected the four corners of the sign and mapped them to a rectangle. The target proportions use an approximate measurement of 43 by 71 cm from the iPhone Measure app.

# A.4 - Image mosaics

I warped the photos onto a common canvas, keeping a middle frame as the reference for the longer sweeps. Simply overwriting one image with another left obvious seams. I used distance-weighted feathering so that pixels near an image boundary contributed less to the overlap.

The basic mosaics use my bilinear inverse warp and feathering. I also tried exposure correction and a two-band seam blend where moving objects caused noticeable ghosting.

## Building courtyard (The Gateway)

This was a three-image mosaic. My first attempt used a reference image at one end of the sweep, which stretched the opposite side quite badly. Using the middle frame worked better.

This mosaic uses two landscape photos. The 12 matching pairs were selected visually using the tool, with points on buildings, roofs, the stone wall, and railings. People, cars, and clouds were left out.

Projecting everything into the right photo's plane made the canvas much too large. I split the relative homography with a matrix square root and warped both photos toward a shared plane between the views. This changed the output plane without changing the matching points.

## Cylindrical projection

For the extra part, I tried projecting the mosaics onto a cylinder instead of a plane. The planar versions stretched the ends of the wider scenes. I used an approximate focal length for each photo set and sampled directly from the original images, rather than rewarping an already stitched mosaic.

<p>For horizontal cylindrical position \(u\), I used \(\theta=(u-c_x)/f\) to find the corresponding viewing angle. I then mapped that ray back through the inverse homography to sample each photo.</p>
